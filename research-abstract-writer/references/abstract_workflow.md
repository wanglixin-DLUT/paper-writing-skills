# Abstract Drafting Workflow

Use this workflow after reading `writing_blueprint.md` when available.

For deep learning projects, also read `content_analysis.md` when available. Use it for technical facts and evidence; use `writing_blueprint.md` for target-reader strategy.

## Required Inputs

Minimum useful inputs:

- Target journal, venue, or conference.
- Word limit and required headings.
- Manuscript type.
- Central contribution.
- Study aim or question.
- Method or material in the level of detail relevant to reader triage.
- Key result, preferably with decisive numbers if they matter.
- Implication, application, or value.
- Main limitation or uncertainty if it affects interpretation.

If the user provides a full draft, extract these inputs before rewriting.

## Blueprint Handoff

From `writing_blueprint.md`, use:

- `Reader And Purpose` to decide how much background to include.
- `Central Contribution` to determine the abstract's take-home message.
- `Global Narrative Arc` to preserve logical flow.
- `Section Move Map > Abstract` to follow target-paper conventions.
- `Style And Language Decisions` to keep terminology, acronyms, voice, and sentence density stable.
- `Claims, Certainty, And Boundaries` to prevent overclaiming.
- `Cross-Section Consistency` to avoid conflict with title, results, and discussion.

If blueprint guidance conflicts with journal instructions, follow the journal instructions and mention the adjustment.

From `content_analysis.md`, use:

- Executive technical summary to identify what the project actually does.
- Contribution candidates to choose the abstract's central value.
- Experiment and result map to select the key result.
- Claim boundaries to avoid unsupported novelty or performance claims.
- File and artifact map only when provenance matters; do not expose file paths in the abstract unless the user asks.

## Drafting Steps

1. State the reader problem.
   - Use one sentence only when broad readers need context.
   - Skip or compress background for highly specialized, short, or methods-forward abstracts.

2. Identify the challenge, gap, uncertainty, limitation, or unmet need.
   - This gives the study a reason to exist.
   - Avoid dramatic language unless the evidence and field convention support it.

3. State what the study does.
   - Use present simple for what the paper presents, reports, proposes, or investigates.
   - Use active voice when contribution ownership might otherwise be unclear.

4. Include method or materials selectively.
   - Include design, dataset, model, sample, system, or experiment only when needed for reader triage or credibility.
   - Avoid procedural detail unless the method is the contribution.

5. Report the central result.
   - Prefer the strongest result that directly supports the central contribution.
   - Include numbers when they carry the claim or prevent vagueness.
   - Do not list secondary results unless they change the reader's decision to read.

6. State contribution, implication, or application.
   - Make value explicit but defensible.
   - Use cautious language for early-stage, indirect, limited, or extrapolated implications.

7. Compress.
   - Combine compatible moves: study action plus contribution, method plus result, result plus implication.
   - Remove duplicated background, excessive method detail, secondary achievements, and repeated modifiers.
   - Preserve links between sentences so the text remains readable.

## Tense And Voice Defaults

- Background or established knowledge: present simple.
- What the paper does: present simple.
- What the authors did or used: past simple.
- Results: past simple or present simple, depending on target-paper convention and immediacy.
- Contribution or value: present simple or present perfect.
- Potential implications: modal verbs or cautious verbs when needed.

Use these as defaults only. Target-paper and journal conventions can override them.

## Draft Review Passes

Run these passes in order:

1. Contribution pass: Can a fast reader identify the central value?
2. Evidence pass: Is every claim supported by the reported result?
3. Reader pass: Is there enough context and method detail to decide relevance?
4. Ambiguity pass: Are `this`, `it`, `which`, tense, passive voice, or acronyms unclear?
5. Compression pass: Does every word help the abstract do its job?
6. Fit pass: Does it match word limit, headings, and target venue?
