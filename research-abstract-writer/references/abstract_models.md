# Abstract Models

Choose a model based on target journal instructions, target-paper conventions, and the user's manuscript type.

## Generic Research Abstract Model

Use this flexible move set:

1. Significance or essential background.
2. Challenge, problem, gap, uncertainty, or unmet need.
3. What the paper or study does.
4. Method, materials, data, model, or design.
5. Key result.
6. Interpretation, contribution, implication, or application.
7. Optional mapping to existing knowledge or broader relevance.

Not every abstract needs every move. The most common compression strategy is to combine adjacent moves.

## Standard Single-Paragraph Abstract

Best for many STEMM journals.

Pattern:

- 1 sentence background or problem.
- 1 sentence study action or aim.
- 1-2 sentences method and central result.
- 1 sentence contribution, implication, or application.

Use when:

- The journal expects one paragraph.
- The abstract must be fast and scannable.
- The article has a clear central contribution.

Watch for:

- Overloading method details.
- Ending with a vague benefit.
- Hiding the central result too late.

## Structured Abstract

Use the journal's required headings exactly. Common heading sets include:

- Background, Methods, Results, Conclusions.
- Objective, Methods, Results, Significance.
- Importance, Objective, Design, Results, Conclusions.
- Purpose, Methods, Findings, Implications.

Strategy:

- Background: reader problem and gap.
- Objective or Aim: precise study action.
- Methods: only design-level detail needed for credibility.
- Results: central evidence, with numbers where meaningful.
- Conclusions: defensible contribution and implication.

Do not let headings create repetition. If the objective is obvious from the background, keep it short.

## Significance-Linked Abstract

Use when the journal also requires a significance statement, highlights, key points, or research-in-context panel.

Strategy:

- Let the abstract carry the technical narrative.
- Let the significance statement or highlights carry broader value, if available.
- Avoid repeating the same contribution in identical wording across all summary elements.
- Preserve consistent terminology across abstract, highlights, and title.

## Graphical-Abstract-Adjacent Summary

Use when a graphical abstract or visual summary exists.

Strategy:

- The text abstract still needs to stand alone.
- Do not rely on the image to explain the method or result.
- Align the final sentence with the visual's main message.
- Keep visual labels and abstract terminology consistent.

## Conference Abstract

Use when the abstract is submitted before the full paper or presentation.

Strategy:

- State the research question or problem early.
- Include method and expected or completed results as allowed by the venue.
- Be transparent if results are preliminary.
- Emphasize novelty and fit to the conference theme.

Watch for:

- Claiming finality before results are complete.
- Using journal-article density when the audience is broader.

## Methods-Forward Abstract

Use when the method, tool, dataset, instrument, model, or pipeline is the main contribution.

Move emphasis:

1. Existing methodological limitation.
2. New method/tool/dataset and what it enables.
3. Validation design.
4. Performance or comparison result.
5. Use case, availability, or application.

## Results-Forward Abstract

Use when the finding is more important than the method.

Move emphasis:

1. Field problem or open question.
2. Study design in brief.
3. Main result first or early.
4. Interpretation.
5. Implication or boundary.

## Application-Forward Abstract

Use when the target readership values use, deployment, translation, policy, or design implications.

Move emphasis:

1. Practical need.
2. Existing barrier.
3. Study action.
4. Evidence of improvement or feasibility.
5. Application and constraints.
