# Introduction Models

Use these models as flexible menus, not rigid templates. Target-paper conventions and journal instructions can override the generic structure.

## Generic Introduction Model

1. Establish the importance of the topic or field.
   - Give the reader a reason to care.
   - Provide essential background facts.
   - Present the general problem area or current research focus.

2. Map previous and current research.
   - Select prior work that explains how the field reached the current problem.
   - Group studies by approach, model, dataset, limitation, chronology, or proximity to the user's study.
   - Show contribution and limitation, not just author names.

3. Locate the gap, problem, motivation, hypothesis, or opportunity.
   - State what remains unknown, unresolved, inefficient, limited, untested, unreliable, expensive, brittle, narrow, or difficult.
   - Keep the language respectful and impersonal.
   - Make the gap current; avoid implying a gap still exists if recent work may have solved it.

4. Describe the present paper.
   - State what the study/paper does.
   - Optionally preview aim, method, dataset, model, result, contribution, or paper structure.
   - Include contribution language only when it is supported by evidence.

## Deep Learning Paper Variant

For deep learning papers, the move plan often becomes:

1. Task importance and application setting.
2. Current technical challenge or evaluation bottleneck.
3. Prior method families and their limitations.
4. Specific unresolved limitation shown by baselines, datasets, or deployment constraints.
5. Present method or model design at a high level.
6. Main contribution and evidence preview.

Typical gap types:

- Accuracy or robustness limitation.
- Generalization to new domains, datasets, or distribution shifts.
- Efficiency, memory, latency, or deployment constraint.
- Data scarcity, annotation cost, noise, imbalance, or weak labels.
- Interpretability, calibration, uncertainty, or reliability.
- Missing ablation, benchmark, dataset, or evaluation protocol.
- Inability of existing methods to capture a specific structure or signal.

## Short Introduction Model

Use for letters, short papers, and strict venues:

1. Field importance in 1-2 sentences.
2. Immediate limitation or gap.
3. Prior approaches in a compressed research map.
4. Present study and contribution.

## Long Introduction Model

Use for complex interdisciplinary work:

1. Broad context.
2. Essential definitions or reader orientation.
3. Multiple strands of prior work.
4. Synthesis of what those strands leave unresolved.
5. Present study.
6. Contributions, scope, and sometimes paper organization.

## Results-Preview Introduction

Some target journals allow or expect a brief result preview near the end.

Use when:

- Target papers commonly preview major results in the Introduction.
- The result helps clarify why the method matters.
- The claim is directly supported by the paper.

Avoid when:

- The Introduction becomes a second abstract.
- Results are complex and need Methods/Results context.
- The preview creates an overclaim before evidence is shown.

## Contribution-List Ending

Use only if target papers in the venue often end the Introduction with explicit contributions.

Keep contribution lists:

- Short.
- Evidence-based.
- Parallel in structure.
- Consistent with `content_analysis.md`.
- Distinct from implementation details.

If the venue does not use contribution lists, prefer a compact final paragraph.
